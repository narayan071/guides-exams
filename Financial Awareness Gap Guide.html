Chapter 1 · Q92

Cheques and other negotiable instruments

Start here. A cheque is a written order from an account holder to their bank: "pay this amount to this person." Three parties are involved. The drawer is the account holder who writes the cheque. The drawee is the bank that pays. The payee is the person who receives the money. Cheques are covered by the Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881 (NI Act), which recognises three negotiable instruments: the cheque, the bill of exchange and the promissory note.

How cheques differ by who can collect them

TypeHow you spot itWhat it means
Bearer chequeThe words "or bearer" are printed and not struck outWhoever holds it can collect the money. Risky if lost.
Order cheque"Bearer" is struck out; reads "or order"Payable to the named payee, or to someone the payee names by signing the back (endorsing).
Open (uncrossed) chequeNo lines across itThe payee can take cash across the bank counter.
Crossed chequeTwo parallel lines across the faceCannot be cashed over the counter. The money must go into a bank account.
Self cheque"Self" written in the payee lineThe account holder withdraws cash from their own account.

The kinds of crossing

CrossingWhat is writtenEffect
General crossingTwo parallel lines. Sometimes "& Co." between them.Must be collected through any bank account. This is what the question was about.
Special crossingThe name of a bank written between the lines or across the chequeCan be collected only through that named bank.
"Account Payee" crossingThe words "Account Payee" or "A/c Payee" between the linesMoney must be credited only to the payee's account. In practice it cannot be transferred by endorsement. This is the only crossing for which "cannot be endorsed" is true.
"Not Negotiable" crossingThe words "Not Negotiable" between the linesThe cheque can still be transferred, but the new holder gets no better right than the person who passed it on (Section 130, NI Act).

Endorsement: signing the back to pass a cheque on

  • Blank endorsement: only a signature. The cheque then becomes payable to whoever holds it.
  • Special (full) endorsement: the signature plus the name of the new payee ("Pay to Ravi Kumar").
  • Restrictive endorsement: limits what can be done with it ("Pay to Ravi Kumar only").
  • Conditional endorsement: payment depends on a condition ("Pay to Ravi if he completes the work").

Dates and validity

  • Validity: a cheque is valid for 3 months from its date (RBI rule since 1 April 2012).
  • Stale (out-of-date) cheque: older than 3 months. The bank will not pay it.
  • Post-dated cheque: carries a future date. It cannot be paid before that date.
  • Antedated cheque: carries an earlier date than the day it was written. It is valid if still within 3 months.
  • Mutilated cheque: torn or damaged. The bank will not pay without the drawer's confirmation.

Other bank payment instruments (siblings)

InstrumentMeaning
Demand draft (DD)A bank's order, paid for in advance, to another branch to pay the named person. Safer than a cheque because the bank, not an individual, guarantees it.
Banker's cheque / pay orderA cheque drawn by the bank on itself, usually payable in the same city.
Traveller's chequeA prepaid instrument used when travelling, redeemable abroad.
Bill of exchangeA written order from a creditor to a debtor to pay a fixed sum, on demand or on a set date. A cheque is a bill of exchange drawn on a bank and payable on demand.
Promissory noteA written promise by one person to pay another a fixed sum.

When a cheque bounces (dishonour)

  • Common reasons: insufficient funds, signature mismatch, stale cheque, post-dated cheque, account closed, payment stopped by the drawer, overwriting without a fresh signature.
  • Section 138, NI Act: bouncing for insufficient funds is a criminal offence. Punishment: up to 2 years' imprisonment, or a fine up to twice the cheque amount, or both.
  • Process: the payee sends a written notice within 30 days of receiving the bank's return memo. The drawer then has 15 days to pay. If not paid, the payee can file a complaint within one month after that.

How cheques clear

  • MICR (Magnetic Ink Character Recognition): the machine-readable line at the bottom of the cheque. It carries a 9-digit MICR code, which tells the bank the city, bank and branch.
  • CTS (Cheque Truncation System): the physical cheque stops at the collecting bank, and its electronic image travels to the paying bank.
  • Since October 2025, RBI has been moving cheque clearing from batches to continuous clearing within hours. Check the latest phase before the exam.

Chapter 2 · Q86, Q90, Q97, Q110, Q123, Q124

Payments and money transfer

Start here. Money moves between banks through payment systems. Most retail payment systems are run by the National Payments Corporation of India (NPCI), a non-profit company set up in 2008 by RBI and the Indian Banks' Association (IBA). Large-value transfers between banks run on RBI's own systems.

Bank codes

  • IFSC (Indian Financial System Code): 11 characters. The first 4 letters are the bank, the 5th is always 0, and the last 6 identify the branch.
  • MICR (Magnetic Ink Character Recognition) code: 9 digits, printed on cheques.
  • VPA (Virtual Payment Address): the UPI ID, like name@bank.

The transfer systems

SystemFull formHow it worksKey facts
RTGSReal Time Gross SettlementEach payment is settled individually, immediately.Minimum ₹2 lakh. No upper limit. Runs 24x7. Run by RBI.
NEFTNational Electronic Funds TransferPayments are bundled and settled in half-hourly batches.Runs 24x7 since December 2019. RBI sets no upper cap; banks may set their own.
IMPSImmediate Payment ServiceInstant transfer, 24x7, through NPCI.Limit ₹5 lakh per transaction.
UPIUnified Payments InterfaceInstant phone-to-phone payment using a UPI ID. Run by NPCI, launched in 2016.Usually ₹1 lakh per transaction, with higher limits for some categories such as taxes, hospitals and education.
NACHNational Automated Clearing HouseBulk and recurring payments such as salaries, loan EMIs (Equated Monthly Instalments) and dividends.Replaced ECS (Electronic Clearing Service). Run by NPCI.
AePSAadhaar Enabled Payment SystemBanking with just an Aadhaar number, a bank name and a fingerprint or iris scan.Balance enquiry, cash withdrawal, deposit, fund transfer, mini statement. Run by NPCI.
BBPSBharat Bill Payment SystemOne platform for paying electricity, water, gas, telephone and other bills.Run by NPCI.
NETC / FASTagNational Electronic Toll CollectionAutomatic toll payment from a prepaid tag on the windscreen.Run by NPCI.

NPCI products

  • RuPay: India's own domestic card network, launched in 2012. International networks are Visa and Mastercard.
  • BHIM (Bharat Interface for Money): a UPI app launched by NPCI on 30 December 2016. That is why Q123's answer is NPCI, not RBI.
  • Google Pay, PhonePe, Paytm: UPI apps run by private companies. They are called TPAPs (Third-Party Application Providers) and need a partner bank called a PSP (Payment Service Provider) bank.
  • Flex by Google Pay: a RuPay credit card that works on UPI, launched in December 2025 with Axis Bank.

Other payment terms

  • Micro ATM: a handheld device used by a bank's local agent (business correspondent) in villages.
  • PPI (Prepaid Payment Instrument): a wallet or prepaid card holding stored value. RBI classifies them as closed, semi-closed or open systems.
  • CBDC (Central Bank Digital Currency): the digital rupee (e₹), piloted in 2022: wholesale from 1 November and retail from 1 December.
  • Payments bank: a bank that accepts deposits (cap ₹2 lakh per customer) and offers payments, but does not lend.
  • PSS Act (Payment and Settlement Systems Act, 2007): the law that gives RBI authority over payment systems.
  • MANI (Mobile Aided Note Identifier): an RBI app that tells a visually impaired person the denomination of a note held up to the camera.
Trap: NPCI runs UPI, IMPS, NACH, AePS, RuPay, BHIM and BBPS. RBI runs RTGS and (with NPCI) oversees them all. If a question asks who "launched" a retail payment product, the answer is usually NPCI.

Chapter 3 · Q87, Q88, Q98, Q103, Q108

RBI and monetary policy

Start here. The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) is India's central bank. It was set up on 1 April 1935 under the RBI Act, 1934, is headquartered in Mumbai, and was nationalised on 1 January 1949. Its governor since December 2024 is Sanjay Malhotra. "Monetary policy" means RBI controlling how much money and credit is in the economy, mainly to keep prices stable.

What RBI does

  • Issues currency notes (except ₹1 notes and coins, which the government issues).
  • Banker to the central and state governments.
  • Banker to banks: banks keep reserves with RBI.
  • Regulates and supervises banks and non-banking financial companies (NBFCs).
  • Keeps the country's foreign exchange reserves.
  • Lender of last resort (LOLR): gives emergency liquidity to eligible banks that cannot borrow elsewhere. This is Q108's answer.
  • Runs the payment system (Chapter 2).

The policy tools

ToolFull form and meaningCurrent setting
Repo rateThe rate at which RBI lends short-term to banks against government securities. "Repo" is short for repurchase agreement.5.50% (from 7 Oct 2026)
Reverse repoThe rate at which RBI borrows from banks. It absorbs surplus cash.Now secondary to SDF
SDFStanding Deposit Facility. Banks park surplus cash with RBI without needing to give government securities. It is the floor of the rate corridor.5.25% (repo − 0.25)
MSFMarginal Standing Facility. Banks borrow overnight in an emergency at a penal rate. It is the ceiling of the corridor.5.75% (repo + 0.25)
Bank RateThe rate at which RBI lends to banks without needing collateral. It equals the MSF rate.5.75%
CRRCash Reserve Ratio. The share of a bank's net demand and time liabilities (NDTL) that it must keep as cash with RBI. No interest is paid on it.3% as of the 2025 cut; check for changes
SLRStatutory Liquidity Ratio. The share of NDTL a bank must hold in cash, gold or approved government securities.18%
OMOOpen Market Operations. RBI buys government securities to put money in, or sells them to take money out.Used as needed
LAFLiquidity Adjustment Facility. The daily window through which RBI injects or absorbs money: repo and reverse repo (and SDF and MSF). This is Q98.Daily

How to think about the direction

  • Inflation too high or the rupee weak: RBI raises the repo rate. Borrowing gets costlier, spending falls, prices cool. (Its October 2026 hike followed this logic.)
  • Growth weak and inflation low: RBI cuts the repo rate. Loans get cheaper.
  • The repo rate sits between SDF and MSF. The gap between them (0.50%) is called the policy corridor.
  • Stance: accommodative (ready to cut), neutral (open either way), or withdrawal of accommodation (leaning to tighten). The stance was neutral as of August 2026. Check the October statement.
  • Qualitative tools: moral suasion (persuading banks), margin requirements and selective credit control.

The Monetary Policy Committee (MPC)

  • Set up in 2016 by an amendment to the RBI Act. It decides the repo rate.
  • Six members: three from RBI (the Governor, who chairs, a Deputy Governor and one more RBI officer) and three external members appointed by the central government for four years.
  • Meetings: the Act requires at least four a year. RBI holds six, roughly every two months. Q87's trap is the number six.
  • Decisions are by majority. The Governor has the casting vote in a tie. The quorum is four members.

Inflation and its target

  • CPI (Consumer Price Index): the price of a typical household basket. It is published monthly by the National Statistics Office (NSO). The MPC targets CPI.
  • WPI (Wholesale Price Index): wholesale prices, published by the Office of the Economic Adviser, DPIIT (Department for Promotion of Industry and Internal Trade).
  • Core inflation: inflation excluding food and fuel.
  • Flexible Inflation Targeting (FIT): adopted in 2016. The target is 4% with a band of ±2% (so 2% to 6%). The government renewed it in March 2026 for 2026–31.
  • If inflation stays outside the band for three straight quarters, RBI must explain why to the government.
  • Terms: inflation (prices rising), deflation (prices falling), disinflation (prices still rising, but slower), stagflation (high inflation with slow growth).
Trap: Q98's "equity underwriting" has nothing to do with liquidity. Equity underwriting is a securities market activity (Chapter 4). If a monetary policy question lists unrelated sectors, pick the option that mentions repo or reverse repo.

Chapter 4 · Q85, Q112, Q118, Q119

IPOs, ASBA, money market and government securities

Start here. When a company wants to raise money from the public by selling shares, it makes an IPO (Initial Public Offer). Shares sold for the first time are in the primary market. Later trading of those shares between investors is in the secondary market (the stock exchanges). The regulator is SEBI (Securities and Exchange Board of India), set up in 1988 and made a statutory body in 1992.

ASBA (Application Supported by Blocked Amount)

  • When you apply for an IPO, your money is blocked in your own bank account, not paid to the company.
  • It is debited only if shares are allotted to you. If you get no allotment, or part of it, the money is unblocked.
  • Introduced by SEBI in 2008. It stops investors losing interest on money tied up in refunds.
  • Retail investors can apply through UPI as the ASBA mode, by approving a mandate on their phone.

Types of share issue (siblings)

TermMeaning
IPO (Initial Public Offer)A company sells shares to the public for the first time.
FPO (Follow-on Public Offer)An already listed company issues more shares to the public.
Rights issueNew shares offered only to existing shareholders, usually at a discount.
OFS (Offer for Sale)Promoters or existing holders sell shares they already own. The company gets no money.
QIP (Qualified Institutional Placement)A listed company raises money quickly from big institutional investors.
Private placementShares sold to a small chosen group, not the public.
Bonus issueFree extra shares to existing holders from the company's reserves.

Market infrastructure

  • Stock exchanges: BSE (Bombay Stock Exchange, index Sensex) and NSE (National Stock Exchange, index Nifty 50).
  • Demat account: an electronic account that holds shares. Two depositories keep these: NSDL (National Securities Depository Limited) and CDSL (Central Depository Services (India) Limited).
  • Retail IPO category: bids up to ₹2 lakh.
  • Trades settle on T+1, meaning one working day after the trade.

Money market and capital market

The money market deals in short-term lending of up to one year. The capital market deals in longer-term money (shares and bonds).

TREPS (Tri-Party Repo)Short-term collateralised lendingOvernight and short term. It replaced CBLO (Collateralised Borrowing and Lending Obligation).

Government securities (G-secs)

  • When you buy a G-sec, you are lending money to the government. This is Q112's answer. It carries no default risk.
  • T-bills: short term (up to 1 year), sold at a discount and redeemed at face value.
  • Dated G-secs: long term (5 to 40 years), with a fixed interest rate (coupon) paid twice a year.
  • SDLs (State Development Loans): bonds issued by state governments.
  • CMBs (Cash Management Bills): very short-term government bills.
  • RBI auctions G-secs for the government. Since November 2021, the RBI Retail Direct portal lets ordinary investors buy them.

Chapter 5 · Q93, Q96, Q109, Q111, Q117

KYC, anti-money laundering, ombudsman and customer rights

Start here. Banks must know who their customers are, to stop fraud and money laundering. They must also give customers a way to complain. This chapter covers the identity rules (KYC), the money laundering rules (PMLA), the RBI complaint scheme (ombudsman), and two international tax rules (FATCA and CRS).

KYC (Know Your Customer)

  • RBI's KYC Master Direction (2016) requires banks to verify identity and address before opening an account, and to monitor it afterwards.
  • OVD (Officially Valid Document): passport, driving licence, proof of possession of an Aadhaar number, voter's identity card, NREGA (National Rural Employment Guarantee Act) job card signed by a state officer, and a letter from the NPR (National Population Register).
  • Not OVDs: ration card (Q96's answer), PAN card (it is needed separately, but is not an OVD), college ID, bank passbook.
  • Deemed OVDs for address: a recent utility bill (not older than 2 months), a property or municipal tax receipt, and pension payment orders issued to retired government employees.
  • Small account: for people without OVDs. It has a balance cap of ₹50,000, annual credits capped at ₹1 lakh, and a validity of 12 months, extendable by another 12.
  • CKYC (Central KYC): a shared record held by CKYCRR (Central KYC Records Registry), which issues a 14-digit KIN (KYC Identification Number). It is run under CERSAI (Central Registry of Securitisation Asset Reconstruction and Security Interest of India).
  • e-KYC uses Aadhaar OTP (One-Time Password) authentication. V-CIP (Video-based Customer Identification Process) is video KYC.
  • Periodic updating: high-risk customers every 2 years, medium-risk every 8 years, low-risk every 10 years.
  • PEP (Politically Exposed Person): a senior public figure, treated as higher risk, with EDD (Enhanced Due Diligence).

Anti-money laundering (AML)

  • Money laundering means making illegal money look legal. Its three stages are placement (getting the money into the system), layering (moving it around to hide the source) and integration (bringing it back as clean money).
  • PMLA (Prevention of Money Laundering Act, 2002): in force since 2005. Banks report to FIU-IND (Financial Intelligence Unit–India).
  • CTR (Cash Transaction Report): for cash transactions above ₹10 lakh in a month. STR (Suspicious Transaction Report): for anything that looks suspicious, whatever the amount.
  • CFT (Combating the Financing of Terrorism) goes with AML.
  • FATF (Financial Action Task Force): the global AML and CFT standard-setter, based in Paris, set up in 1989. It keeps "grey" and "black" lists of countries.
  • Shell bank (Q109): a bank with no physical presence in any country and no affiliation to a regulated financial group. Banks are barred from dealing with them. This is different from a shell company, which exists on paper with no real business.

The RBI Ombudsman

  • Before 2021, there were three separate schemes: the Banking Ombudsman Scheme 2006, the Ombudsman Scheme for Non-Banking Financial Companies (NBFCs) 2018 and the Ombudsman Scheme for Digital Transactions 2019.
  • On 12 November 2021, RBI merged them into the RB-IOS (Reserve Bank – Integrated Ombudsman Scheme), called "One Nation One Ombudsman". This is Q111's answer. In 2022 credit information companies were added.
  • How to use it: first complain to the bank. If it does not reply within 30 days, or you are not satisfied, go to the RBI Ombudsman (online through the RBI complaint portal, or by post to the CRPC, Centralised Receipt and Processing Centre, in Chandigarh).
  • 2021 limits: up to ₹20 lakh for consequential loss and up to ₹1 lakh for harassment and mental anguish. The paper's options (Q93) follow this scheme.
  • 2026 scheme: a revised scheme took effect on 1 July 2026. Reports put the loss limit at ₹30 lakh and harassment compensation at up to ₹3 lakh.

Customer rights and deposit insurance

  • Charter of Customer Rights: five rights: fair treatment; transparency, fair and honest dealing; suitability; privacy; grievance redress and compensation. The BCSBI (Banking Codes and Standards Board of India) promotes these codes.
  • DICGC (Deposit Insurance and Credit Guarantee Corporation): an RBI subsidiary that insures deposits up to ₹5 lakh per depositor per bank. This is deposit insurance, which is not Basel II Pillar 1 (Chapter 9).

International tax reporting

  • FATCA (Foreign Account Tax Compliance Act): a US law (2010) that makes foreign banks report accounts held by US taxpayers. India signed an agreement with the US in 2015. This is Q117.
  • CRS (Common Reporting Standard): a similar worldwide system from the OECD (Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development), in which countries exchange account information automatically.

Chapter 9 · Q95

Basel norms

Start here. Banks lend out other people's money, so they need their own capital as a cushion for losses. The Basel Committee on Banking Supervision (BCBS), set up in 1974 and hosted by the Bank for International Settlements (BIS) in Basel, sets international standards for how much. These standards are called Basel I, II and III. RBI adapts them for India.
NormYearWhat it did
Basel I1988Focused on credit risk. Minimum capital 8% of risk-weighted assets.
Basel II2004Three pillars (below).
Basel III2010 onwards, after the 2008 financial crisisHigher quality of capital, new buffers, a leverage ratio and liquidity ratios.

The three pillars of Basel II

  • Pillar 1: minimum capital requirement. Banks hold capital against credit risk, market risk and operational risk. This is Q95's answer.
  • Pillar 2: supervisory review. The regulator checks a bank's own capital planning. Tools: ICAAP (Internal Capital Adequacy Assessment Process) and SREP (Supervisory Review and Evaluation Process).
  • Pillar 3: market discipline. Banks must publish disclosures so the market can judge them.

Basel III in plain terms

  • CET1 (Common Equity Tier 1): the best quality capital (equity and retained profits).
  • Tier 1 (going-concern capital: CET1 plus Additional Tier 1 or AT1 bonds) absorbs losses while the bank is running. Tier 2 (subordinated debt and some reserves) absorbs losses only if the bank is wound up.
  • CCB (Capital Conservation Buffer): an extra 2.5% cushion. CCyB (Countercyclical Capital Buffer): an extra buffer, 0 to 2.5%, switched on in boom times.
  • Leverage ratio: a plain cap on how much a bank can borrow compared to its capital.
  • LCR (Liquidity Coverage Ratio): a bank must hold enough high-quality liquid assets to survive 30 days of stress. NSFR (Net Stable Funding Ratio): a measure of stable funding over a year.
  • D-SIBs (Domestic Systemically Important Banks): banks so large that RBI requires extra capital. India's three are SBI, HDFC Bank and ICICI Bank.
  • RWA (risk-weighted assets): a bank's assets weighted by how risky they are. Capital is measured against RWA.

India's requirements

  • CRAR (Capital to Risk-weighted Assets Ratio): minimum 9% (Basel's minimum is 8%). With the 2.5% CCB, it comes to 11.5%.
  • CET1 minimum 5.5%, Tier 1 minimum 7%.
Trap: "Deposit insurance", "financial inclusion", "customer grievance redressal" and "branch licensing" are all real banking topics, but none is a Basel pillar. Deposit insurance is DICGC (Chapter 5).

Chapter 10 · Q89, Q99, Q105, Q106

Foreign remittance, GDP, crypto and company law

Foreign money: the LRS (Liberalised Remittance Scheme)

  • Under the LRS, a resident individual (not a company) can send up to USD 250,000 per financial year abroad. This is Q89's answer. It was USD 25,000 when it started in 2004 and reached USD 250,000 in 2015.
  • Permitted uses include education, travel, medical treatment, gifts and overseas investment. Some uses, such as lottery tickets, are prohibited.
  • The governing law is FEMA (Foreign Exchange Management Act, 1999). Current account transactions (day-to-day payments) are largely free. Capital account transactions (investments, loans) are controlled.
  • FDI (Foreign Direct Investment): lasting investment that gives control or a stake in a business. FPI (Foreign Portfolio Investment): buying shares or bonds without control. ECB (External Commercial Borrowing): a loan raised by an Indian firm abroad.

GDP (Gross Domestic Product)

  • The value of all final goods and services produced inside a country in a period. Nominal GDP uses current prices. Real GDP adjusts for inflation and is the one used for growth rates.
  • GVA (Gross Value Added): GDP minus taxes plus subsidies. It shows production by sector.
  • The NSO (National Statistics Office), under MoSPI (Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation), releases quarterly estimates about two months after each quarter ends.
  • The Indian financial year runs April to March. Q1 is April to June (released at the end of August), Q2 is July to September (end of November), Q3 is October to December (end of February) and Q4 is January to March (end of May).
  • Recent figures: Q2 FY26 (paper): 8.2%. Q1 FY27: 7.8%.

Bitcoin and digital money

  • Bitcoin is a cryptocurrency created in 2009 by a person or group using the name Satoshi Nakamoto. Its record of transactions is a blockchain, a shared ledger no single party controls.
  • Maximum supply: 21 million coins. This is Q105's answer. New coins are created by "mining", and the reward halves roughly every four years.
  • In India: crypto is neither banned nor RBI-regulated money. Profits are taxed at 30% with 1% TDS (Tax Deducted at Source). The tax law calls these assets VDAs (Virtual Digital Assets). RBI has not accepted it as a currency.
  • The official digital currency is the RBI's e₹ (CBDC, Chapter 2).

Strategy: when to attempt

The arithmetic of guessing

Each question has five options. Each wrong answer costs 0.25 marks.

  • Blind guess (5 options): 1/5 × 1 − 4/5 × 0.25 = 0. Pointless on average.
  • After eliminating one option (4 left): 1/4 − 3/4 × 0.25 = +0.0625.
  • After eliminating two (3 left): 1/3 − 2/3 × 0.25 = +0.17.

So attempt whenever you can eliminate at least one option. Skip only when you have no elimination at all.

Time

40 questions in 15 minutes is about 22 seconds each. Take a first pass through the ones you know in seconds, and return to the rest only if time remains.

Study routine

  • Read one chapter a day, then answer its quick checks from memory.
  • Do one timed 40-question set each weekend and score it against the options.
  • For every wrong answer, write one line: the trap, and the chapter it belongs to.
